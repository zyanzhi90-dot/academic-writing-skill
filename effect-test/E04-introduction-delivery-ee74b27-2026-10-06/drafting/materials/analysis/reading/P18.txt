Stability Criterion and Stability Enhancement for a
Thruster-Assisted Underwater Hexapod Robot
Lepeng Chen , Rongxin Cui , Member, IEEE, Weisheng Yan , Chenguang Yang , Fellow, IEEE,
Zhijun Li , Fellow, IEEE, Hui Xu , and Haitao Yu
Abstract—The stability criterion is critical for the design of
legged robots’ motion planning and control algorithms. If these
algorithms cannot theoretically ensure legged robots’ stability,
we need many trials to identify suitable parameters for stable
locomotion. However, most existing stability criteria are tailored
to robots driven solely by legs and cannot be applied to thruster-
assisted legged robots. Here, we propose a stability criterion for a
thruster-assisted underwater hexapod robot by finding maximum
and minimum allowable thruster forces and comparing them with
the current thrusts to check its stability. On this basis, we propose
a method to increase the robot’s stability margin by adjusting
the value of thrusts. This process is called stability enhancement.
The criterion uses the optimization method to transform multiple
variables such as attitude, velocity, acceleration of the robot body,
and the angle and angular velocity of leg joints into one kind of
variable (thrust) to judge the stability directly. In addition, the
stability enhancement method is straightforward to implement
because it only needs to adjust the thrusts. These provide insights
into how multiclass forces such as inertia force, fluid force, thrust,
gravity, and buoyancy affect the robot’s stability.
Index Terms—Stability criterion, stability enhancement, thru-
ster-assisted underwater hexapod robot.
I. INTRODUCTION

because they may suffer from multiclass disturbances while
walking, such as waves, currents, towing force of cable, and
uncertain leg-terrain interaction force [1], [2]. The robot is stable
if it can recover to the preset motion state by itself under the
disturbances. Otherwise, it is unstable. In addition, if motion
planning and control algorithms cannot theoretically ensure
stability, they cannot be applied to other robots directly, and

A. Related Works
The dynamic model can be used to predict the legged robot’s
motion by establishing the relationship between multiclass
forces and the motion states. Knowing the future motions con-
tributes to evaluating the robot’s stability. The model mainly
consists of two types: inverted pendulum model [6], [7], [8], [9]
and force balancing model [10], [11], [12], [13]. The former is
established by simplifying the robot as an inverted pendulum,
and the latter is an equilibrium equation to balance multiclass
forces, e.g., leg-terrain interaction forces, inertia forces, and
other forces.
Inspired by the spring-loaded inverted pendulum (SLIP)
model for ground legged robots, Calisti et al. [6] proposed a
new dynamic model, called underwater spring-loaded inverted

Lagrange method and incorporating the rolling character caused

motion of walking on flat terrain and need an assumption that
the leg-terrain friction should be large enough.
Several attractive force balancing models are developed
in [10], [11], and [12] for ground quadruped robots, that relax
the above flat terrain and sufficient friction assumptions. Calisti
et al. [13] presented a force balancing model for a novel octopus-
inspired underwater robot by considering leg-terrain interaction
forces and drags arising from the sculling motion of the legs.
These models apply only to ground robots with point-contact
legs. Thus, we need to establish a force balancing model for our
robot by considering its unique driving manner and fluid forces.
Several insightful stability criteria are presented for legged
robots and can be divided into three categories: zero-tilting
moment point (ZMP) criterion [14], [15], Poincare return map
criterion [3], [16], [17], and force balancing criterion [18], [19],
[20], [21].
Vukobratovi et al. [14], [15] initially presented the ZMP crite-
rion, which checks the stability by judging whether the ZMP lies
within the support polygon. It has been widely used to evaluate
legged robots’ stability because of its clear physical meaning and
easy-use advantage. Note that this criterion assumes that contact
terrain is flat and leg-terrain friction is adequate. In addition, the
ZMP criterion is a constraint to prevent the robot from tipping
over. It only uses the support legs’ rotating information, ignoring
the swing legs’ information. That said, it overlooks the robots’
alternating support character.
The Poincare return map is a tool to transform the stability
of a periodic orbit to a fixed point’s stability [22], which can
be used to judge periodically walking robots’ stability. Grizzle
et al. [16], [17] extended this criterion to a hybrid dynamics
with impulse effects and applied it to judge the stability of a
three degree-of-freedom (DoF) humanoid robot with leg-terrain
impacts. Fu et al. [3] presented a section-map stability criterion
to check the stability of nonperiodic walking robots.
The force balancing criterion relies on the force-balance prin-
ciple to check whether the robot can tip over or slip. Hirukawa
et al. [18] presented a novel stability criterion for legged robots,
which checks if the sum of the gravity and inertia wrench lies
within the region constructed by noncoplanar contact points.
Although more universal than the ZMP criterion, it is still
based on the sufficient friction assumption. Caron et al. [19]
considered friction and linearized pendulum constraints and
presented a more necessary and sufficient criterion to check the
multicontact stability. Using the static force balancing model
and optimization algorithm, Audren et al. [21] constructed a
robust 3-D region that consists of all feasible center of mass
(CoM) positions by suppressing bounded CoM accelerations.
If the CoM position remains within this region, the robot is
stable. These interesting stability criteria are the only necessary
conditions for stable locomotion. The robot may still be stable
due to its alternating support character when these conditions are
violated.
In addition, the abovementioned criteria are limited to robots
that are only driven by legs and cannot be applied to our robot.
Previously, considering the alternating support character, we
proposed a stability criterion for our robot in [23], extending the
concept of the capture point to a capture domain and determining
stability based on the relationship between the capture and
support domains. However, this criterion is only applicable to
flat terrain walking. Besides, it requires both motion states and
thrust values for stability evaluation, complicating the design of
stability enhancement methods.
According to the difference in principle, the stability en-
hancement methods can be divided into three categories: robotic
functional mechanism designing [24], [25], [26], [27], supernu-
merary tail adding [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], and inner motion
planning and controlling [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38].
The first category focuses on modifying robots’ geometri-
cal and physical properties to buffer the leg-terrain impact or
improve the self-stabilizing capability. Chellapurath et al. [24]
disclosed how leg configuration, net weight, and the nature of
the substrate affect the passive station-keeping performance of
an underwater legged robot and found that the performance
improves with an increase in net weight, a decrease in height, and
an increase in width. In [25] and [26], two novel self-adaptive
toe mechanisms are presented to possess cat claws’ buffer and
hold performance.
The second category involves using the drag or inertia force
generated by the added tail to enhance stability. Zhang et al. [28]
presented a new algorithm to alter the system center of gravity by
adjusting the joint angles of a three DoF manipulator, enhancing
the ability to prevent tip over. Inspired by basilisk lizards, Kim
et al. [29] developed an interesting water-running robot that uses
a two DoF tail to provide drag for enhancing the stability of
yaw and pitch motion. In [30], an elegant optimal controller is
proposed to steer the joints of the added tail to compensate for
the neural delay dynamics for enhancing the human stance’s
stability. Using a deep reinforcement learning method, Huang
et al. [31] created a policy to automatically adjust the behaviors
of the tail to maintain a quadruped robot’s stability.
The third category relies on the stability criterion or bioinspi-
ration to determine control inputs and plan the robot’s desired
motion. In [33] and [34], the ZMP criterion is used to construct
stability constraints integrated into the trajectory optimization
framework to obtain optimal and stable motions for legged
robots. Choi et al. [35] presented a new method to form the
regions of attraction for underactuated legged robots, and it
facilitates the design of the controller for stability enhancement.
Using adaptive central pattern generators, Mehr et al. [36]
presented a novel motion planner to adjust the upper body’s
position for ensuring a lower limb exoskeleton’s postural sta-
bility. Imitating animals’ natural gaits, Humphreys et al. [38]
presented a gait transition method for locomotion proficiency
enhancing.
Despite the abovementioned methods achieving impressive
results in stability enhancement, they are mainly applicable to
robots that allow the addition of tails or robots only driven by
legs. They are challenging to apply to legged robots simultane-
ously driven by legs and thrusters.

This article’s difficulty lies in how to propose the method of
defining static, quasi-dynamic, and dynamic walking motions
for thruster-assisted legged robots. Moreover, how to judge the
robot’s stability by using the defined motions and considering
the alternating support character is another difficulty.
The main contributions can be specified as follows.
1) We establish a force balancing model of our robot by
considering the fluid forces, hip joint’s driving torque
limit, friction limit, and for rough terrain climbing.
2) We define three motions under the force balancing model,
e.g., static, quasi-dynamic, and dynamic walking, and re-
veal the connection associated with the alternating support
character.
3) We present a stability criterion by using an optimization
algorithm to find a feasible set of thrusts, which has a
good necessity due to considering the alternating support
character.
4) We propose a stability enhancement method with clear
physical meanings because the desired stability margin
can be achieved only by adjusting the thruster force ac-
cording to the feasible set.
The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section II
establishes a force balancing model for our robot. Section III
defines three motions under the established model and presents
a model relaxation method to fuse the alternating support charac-
ter. Section IV provides a vertical thrust-based stability criterion
and stability enhancement method. Section V presents a 6-D
thrust vector based stability criterion and stability enhancement
method. Sections VI and VII present simulation and experi-
mental results, respectively. Finally, Section VIII concludes this
article.
II. FORCE BALANCING MODEL
A. Framework of the Force Balancing Model
Assumption 1: The legs have zero mass, and the robot’s CoM
is at the plane’s center point, consisting of six hip joints.
Assumption 2: The shape of the leg is a semicircle, and its
deformation is ignored.
Assumption 3: Friction coefficients between legs and terrain
are constant and identical.
Assumption 4: The drive motors of the six legs are identical.
Since the total mass of the six legs accounts for about 3%
of the robot’s mass, its influence on the robot’s dynamics is
negligible. In addition, the C-shaped legs are made of hard steel.
Their shape deformations are extremely tiny while impacting
terrains. Thus, we ignore the legs’ deformations. The unmodeled

Remark 3: Compared to the definition of the three types of
motions in [39], our definition has the advantage of being close
to the actual situation and can be applied to more universal cases.
The advantages lie in the following three aspects.
1) Unlike the definition in [39], in addition to being relevant
to the gravity and inertia force, our definition is also
relevant to the fluid and thruster forces. Therefore, our
definition is more universal and can be used to define the
motion of thruster-assisted robots and underwater-legged
robots.
2) Different from the definition in [39], which assumes that
leg-terrain friction and joints’ driving torques are large
enough, our definition relaxes these assumptions. That
said, the walking motions sorted by our definition are
closer to the actual situation.
3) Our definition sorts the walking motions by checking
whether feasible leg-terrain contact forces exist. There-
fore, it is convenient to sort the robot’s motion of climbing
rough terrain. In contrast, the definition in [39] needs to
assume that the terrain is flat. In addition, the use of a
contact force-based force balancing model makes it easy
to fuse other kinds of external forces and thrusts.
We may be unable to find the feasible contact forces (solu-
tions) by using optimization methods when the constraints in
(1) and (2) are too strict. Many factors lead to the constraints’
strictness,e.g.,thenumberofsupportlegs,theamountofinertial,
fluid forces, and thrusts. Next, the existence of feasible solutions
when the robot has 1, 2, and at least three support legs will be
discussed, respectively.
1) When the robot has only one support leg, the number of
unknown variables in (1) is 3, and there are three contact
forces at the touchdown point. The number of the equality
constraints in (1) is 6. Thus, (1) is overconstrained. That
said, there is no feasible contact force. In such a case, the
robot belongs to dynamic walking.
2) When the robot has two support legs, the number of
unknown variables in (1) is 6, which is the number of
contact forces at the two touchdown points. The number
of inequality and equality constraints are 4 and 6, respec-
tively. Thus, it is highly likely that a feasible solution for
six variables cannot be found. In such a case, the robot is
highly dynamic walking.
3) When the robot has at least Ns(Ns ≥ 3) support legs, the
number of unknown variables in (1) is 3Ns. The number of
equality constraints in (1) is 6, and the number of inequal-
ity constraints is 2Ns. Thus, when the inertial forces and
the thrusts are within a reasonable range, feasible solutions
in 3Ns variables can be found, and the robot may belong
to static or dynamic walking.
When it belongs to dynamic walking, and its swing leg does
not switch to the support one, the legged robot must fall due to
tip over. However, the legged robot relies on the legs’ alternate
support character to realize the walking. It is still possible to
make the swing leg switch to the support leg timely by gait
planning, and the robot remains stable.

IV. VERTICAL THRUST-BASED STABILITY CRITERION AND
ENHANCEMENT
We present a criterion to judge the robot’s stability by check-

vertical forces. On this basis, we propose a method to enhance
the stability by changing the vertical thrust’s value.
When the vertical thrust is too large, the robot may fall due
to the support leg’s torque limit. When the vertical thrust is too
small,theforwardandlateralcontactforcesmaynotbesufficient

support leg slipping. Setting the appropriate vertical thrusts is
necessary so the robot will not fall or slip. We need to find the
maximum/minimum vertical thrusts that can be applied to the

C. Robust Stability Criterion and Enhancement
The force balancing model in (1) is not accurate enough
because it ignores some dynamics, such as legs’ inertia forces,
high-order fluid forces, and external disturbances caused by
ocean currents and the electrical cable.
The difficulty of providing an accurate enough robot model
lies in the following three aspects.
1) The coupled dynamics between the legs and robot body
increase the difficulty of modeling. They are challenging
to decouple for stability analysis.
2) Unlike the torpedo-like robots with regular shapes and
high locomotion velocity, our robot has an irregular open-
frame structure and slow locomotion velocity. It is diffi-
cult to obtain accurate fluid forces for such a robot and
scenario, especially the high-order fluid forces.
3) Ocean current varies in size from place to place, which is
challenging to obtain a priori or measure online.
The unmodeled dynamics lead to an inaccurate convex set
that may result in an inaccurate stability assessment. Thus, we
formulate a stricter leg-terrain friction constraint to suppress
uncertainties and present a robust stability criterion that will
result in an accurate stability assessment.

C. Discussion
We find the results between simulations in Section VI-C and
experiments are different, and they arise from the differences
in the chosen parameters, such as the robot’s mass, friction
coefficient, and torque limit. Then, we will introduce the two
reasons accounting for the parameters’ difference.
1) In the experiment, the rough terrain’s surface is made of an
iron sheet, which is only three millimetres thick and can
deform slightly under pressure. The buffer caused by the
deformation can reduce the leg-terrain impact forces. The
simulation’s terrain is hard and can result in larger leg-
terrain impact forces. Then, in the simulation, we reduce
the robot’s mass to 5 kg because the smaller mass can
result in smaller impact forces, generating smaller inertia
forces that can be suppressed easily.
2) Since the mass in the simulation is small, we only need
to use much smaller torque limits to steer the legs. Thus,
the torque limit in simulation is set as 2.5 N · m, which is
much smaller than the value of 16 N · m in the experiment.

VIII. CONCLUSION
In this article, we have presented a new method to define
the three types of walking motion under the established force
balancing model. In addition, we have proposed two criteria to
judge the stability of our thruster-assisted underwater hexapod
robots by checking whether the current thrust belongs to the
allowable region, where the region is calculated by the optimiza-
tion method. We have verified our criterion’s effectiveness and
clarified how factors, such as relaxations, maximum torques,
and friction coefficients, affect the stability through multiple
comparative simulations and experiments. On this basis, we
have presented a method to enhance stability by changing the
value of the vertical thrust. Simulation and experiment results
have demonstrated that our method enhances the robot’s stability
probability by 15%–20%.
